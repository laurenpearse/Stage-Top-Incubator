\section{ITO Coated Glass}
\label{app:ito-options}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------

ITO glass was preferred from the start, but no South African supplier stocks it in a usable size, and the overseas suppliers were either too expensive or unclear about selling single pieces. A backup plan was worked out: ITO-coated PET film laminated between two sheets of plain glass, with copper tape busbars. Konshen Glass then quoted R\,2\,560 for five custom-cut pieces with a 10 to 15 day lead time (Table~\ref{tab:ito-options}), which brought real ITO glass within budget. The film was not needed, and the calculation in Section~\ref{sec:temperature-design} later showed it would not have given enough power at 12\,V anyway, since it only comes in a 50\,$\Omega$/sq grade.

The glass is 150\,mm $\times$ 112\,mm $\times$ 1.1\,mm, which is the plate footprint plus about 12\,mm on each side for the busbars. Thinner glass breaks too easily at this size. Five pieces were ordered because a broken piece could not be replaced locally within the project time.

Silver paste and evaporated metal are the usual busbar materials, but both need equipment that was not available \citep{konshen_ito_2026}. Copper tape was chosen over conductive silver adhesive because it is cheaper, available locally, already has adhesive on it, and carries far more current than the 21\,W panel draws. Three sources were priced at R\,49, R\,385 and R\,96, and the Konshen tape was chosen so that the tape and the glass came from the same supplier.

Table~\ref{tab:temp-sensor-options} lists the temperature sensors that were considered. The Micro Robotics PT100 is by far the cheapest, but it gives an accuracy of 0.5\% without stating a tolerance class, so it cannot be compared against the accuracy target. The 60\,cm PT100 is only 0.006\,\textdegree{}C more accurate than the chosen PT1000 at three times the price, which is not worth it for a reference used to calibrate thermistors that are accurate to $\pm$0.2\,\textdegree{}C.

The two converters were chosen to suit the sensors. The PT1000 is read by a MAX31865 (R\,245), which is made for RTDs and uses SPI \citep{maxim_max31865_2015}. Connecting four thermistors straight to the microcontroller would use four analogue pins and depend on its built-in converter, so an ADS1115 (R\,245) is used instead. It has 16 bits, four channels and programmable gain, and uses I\textsuperscript{2}C \citep{ti_ads1115_2018}. An 18-bit MCP3424 was rejected because it costs more and the accuracy is limited by the thermistor, not the converter.

\section{CO\textsubscript{2} Supply and Valves}
\label{app:co2-valve-options}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------

From the Air Products 2026 price list, a K cylinder holding about 31\,kg costs about R\,1\,708 in total, and the smaller F cylinder costs R\,1\,758 to R\,2\,647. The smaller cylinder costs more for less gas because the rental, delivery and admin charges are fixed. Buying a regulator and a solenoid separately costs at least R\,2\,987, while one assembly that also includes the needle and check valves costs R\,1\,628.

Table~\ref{tab:co2-valve-options} lists every supply and valve option. The regulator inlet was listed as G5/8, which is not the G1/4 Type 30 fitting used on South African cylinders. The part that arrived was Type 30, so the listing was wrong and no adapter was needed on the regulator side. The SodaStream adapter is still needed, because a SodaStream cylinder does not have a Type 30 valve. A separate check valve was priced at R\,98 but not ordered, because the regulator assembly already has one.

The mains solenoid is switched by a 5\,V relay module rated 10\,A at 250\,V, which keeps the logic side separate from the mains.

The gas line uses 6\,mm push-in fittings throughout, because that is the size the main components use, so any section can be replaced without an adapter. Two RS PRO 6\,mm bulkhead fittings (R\,134 each) take the gas line through the chamber wall. A bulkhead clamps onto both sides of the wall, so it holds up to the chamber being opened many times, which a tube through a drilled hole would not. Two RS PRO PG9 brass cable glands (R\,60 each) take the sensor wires through the wall. The wires are potted where they pass through, because a gland is made to grip one round cable and a bundle of thin wires is not round.

Table~\ref{tab:co2-sensor-options} lists the gas sensors that were considered. The ExplorIR-M-E-100 is rated for $-$25 to 55\,\textdegree{}C and 0 to 95\% RH (non-condensing), uses UART and draws about 1\,mA \citep{gss_explorir_2021}. Two cheap sensors were added late in testing. When the SCD30 stopped working, a DHT22 took over the humidity measurement for the long run in Section~\ref{sec:humidity}, and when the room thermistor was moved onto the glass, a BMP280 took over the room temperature. Both were bought locally on the day for R\,64 in total, and both are good enough for channels that are measured but not controlled.

\section{Power}
\label{app:power-options}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------

A 12\,V, 10\,A, 120\,W wall adapter (R\,295) powers the system, with a 12\,V barrel plug (R\,33) on the end of the lead. An XL4016 buck converter (R\,58), rated for 4 to 38\,V in and up to 8\,A, steps the 12\,V down for the microcontroller and sensors. Its current rating is far above what the logic side draws, which leaves margin for the switching noise the heaters put on the supply.
